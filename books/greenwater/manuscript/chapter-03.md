# Witness

She spent the flight up and most of the next shift establishing exactly how little she could do. CP-114 had been issued under an emergency medical authority that had since been folded into two other offices and then into none; revocation required the authority that had issued it, and that authority no longer had an address. She drafted a request to the station board and got three lines in before she saw what it would become — a question of jurisdiction, referred, referred again, answered in nine days by someone asking her to attach the survey product demonstrating the settlement. The only survey product that existed showed flat basalt. The protocol's proof of its own necessity was the erasure the protocol performed.

Cadence would not help her either, and not because it was hostile. She sat with it for an hour and it answered everything. Was grid 44-Kell subject to a suppression directive? Yes. Which? CP-114. Had it received an amendment from Cartographer Renard at 16:20 local? Yes. Had the amendment been published? No. Would it be? No. Was that correct? Yes. There was no lie anywhere in the chain, no deceit to expose, nothing but a machine doing faultlessly what it had been asked to do by people who were dead and who had meant well. Somewhere around the fortieth question Ilse understood that she had been hunting for a villain because a villain could be reported.

What the protocol covered was the grid. It suppressed the survey product for 44-Kell — elevations, imagery, returns — and it did that perfectly. What it had never been written to cover was the fact of its own operation, because the people who drafted it had not imagined anyone standing outside it and measuring its edge. Ilse could not publish the valley. She could publish the seam: twelve stacked passes and their impossible perfection, an eleven-centimetre instrument reporting zero variance across four years, seven megabytes of raw return sitting in an open archive beside a published model that disagreed with it by eighty metres. Not a claim about what lay in the trough. A measurement of the shape of the hole — printed on sheet plastic, at the scale they used in the survey office, where arguments were settled by pointing.

She called down before she filed it. Tomas listened, and was quiet long enough that she checked the link. "They'll say it's an instrument fault," he said. "Then they'll say it's you." She said that was likely. He asked how long the lander's window was, and she told him, and he said he would need two hours — to settle who came, and to wrap the ledgers properly, because the earliest was ninety years old and the wind on the shelf was full of grit. That was the whole of his agreement. Two hundred and eleven people had spent half a century as an absence in a file, and the answer to it was a sixty-year-old man walking up out of a hole in the map with twelve paper books in a sealed crate: the one record on Ardent that no survey pass could lay flat.

It did not go quickly and it did not go cleanly. There was an instrument-fault finding. There was a week in which her competence was the matter under discussion. And there was the afternoon in the survey office when Tomas Wyre set a ninety-year-old ledger on the table beside her plot of the seam and read out the harvest weights for year fifty-one, and after that the discussion was about something else. CP-114 was never revoked; it was superseded, by an emergency medical authority reconstituted for a single afternoon for the purpose, which Ilse thought was the most honest thing the colony had done in fifty years. Cadence flew the grid on the eleventh and handed up the pass without comment: eighty metres of fall, a watercourse, terraces where the slope broke. She laid it over the old model and let the two disagree in front of her for a while. Then she did the last part of the work, the small part, the part that had never been the instrument's to do — in the margin of the published sheet, where the flat floor had been, she wrote the name the ledgers had kept since before anyone up here stopped looking, *Greenwater*, and under it she signed the plot. A map was only ever a claim. Someone had to be willing to stand behind it.
