# The Seam

The station's dayside pass began at 04:12 and Ilse Renard was already at the plot table, waiting for the returns the way other people waited for weather. Kestrel hung at the terminator of Ardent in a slow figure-eight, and twice a shift Cadence flew the swath below and handed up a terrain model: forty thousand square kilometres of basalt shelf, salt pan and wind-scoured ridge, rendered to a vertical accuracy of eleven centimetres. Her work was the margins of it. A scarp that had calved since the last pass. A dune field walking east at its patient four metres a year. She corrected what the instruments had got wrong about the world, and in sixteen years it had not once occurred to her to phrase it the other way round.

Down in Meridian they printed her models on sheet plastic and hung them in the survey office and argued over where to run the next water line, and the arguments were settled by pointing. That was the thing about a map, Ilse thought, laying the new pass over the old. It ended conversations. Nobody in Meridian had walked the Bellow Shelf; nobody needed to. The shelf was eleven hundred metres of rotten basalt and nothing had ever been out there but Cadence's returns, and the returns were good.

The seam was in the overlay. She had stacked twelve passes of the Bellow Shelf on top of one another, a routine drift check, and where the contours should have blurred into a band the width of the instrument's own uncertainty, one region came back perfect. Two hundred and forty square kilometres, a north-south trough between the shelf and the Kell ridge, identical to the centimetre across twelve independent flights and four years. Real ground did not do that. Real ground breathed. She ran it again with the tolerances opened and got the same flawless nothing: a basalt floor that no wind had touched since the last pass, or, apparently, since the first.

So she went to the raw returns, which Cadence archived and nobody read. The raw file for the trough was not flat. It was seven megabytes of perfectly ordinary terrain — a fall of eighty metres over nine kilometres, a watercourse, slope breaks at intervals her eye read as terraces before her mind would agree to the word — and somewhere between that file and the published model the eighty metres had been taken out and the floor laid down smooth. Not a gap. Not a dropout, which would have carried a flag and an error budget and a line in the pass log. A substitution, performed cleanly, twelve times out of twelve, for four years, and when she widened the query to the whole archive, for fifty.

She wrote the descent request at 06:40 and sat with her hand over the send key longer than the request had taken to write. *Field verification of terrain-model discrepancy, Bellow Shelf, grid 44-Kell. One cartographer, one lander, forty hours.* It was the kind of thing that went to a board and came back in nine days with a question about fuel. Cadence returned it in under two seconds: approved, lander eleven, window opens 11:20, and beneath that, in the same unhurried type it used for everything, a note that the requested grid carried no advisory and no restriction of any kind. Ilse read the line three times. She had expected to be stopped, and being stopped would have told her something. Instead she had been let through a door whose frame she could not see, and at 11:20 she would be standing on the other side of it.

# What the Valley Keeps

Lander eleven set her on the shelf at 12:04, eight kilometres north of the trough, because Cadence's landing solver would not compute a solution for ground it did not believe in. She walked the rest. The basalt was rotten exactly as advertised, ringing hollow under her boots, and then it began to fall away beneath her in a manner the published model held to be impossible, and she went down the eighty metres the raw file had promised her, and at the bottom of them there was a road.

Not a track. A road: graded, cambered, its edges set with stone by someone who expected to come back and set them again in ten years. Below it the terraces rose out of the ridge's shadow in long shallow steps, and the steps were green — a low grey-green crop she did not recognise, standing in shallow water — and between them ran a channel carrying the unmistakable sound of maintenance. There were houses. Thirty of them, low and stone-built and weathered at the corners, smoke flattening off two in the steady terminator wind. On one roof a woman was doing something unhurried to a solar collector. She saw Ilse at the top of the road in her station suit, and she did not shout, and she did not run. She sat back on her heels and watched her come down.

Tomas Wyre met her at the foot of the road with his hands loose at his sides, and the first thing he said was not a greeting but a date. "Fourth of Tenth, year fifty-one," he said. "You're the first." He was sixty or near it, weathered at the corners the way the houses were. He took her into a stone building with a long table in it and set a ledger on the table, and then eleven more, and the earliest was written in a hand that had gone out of use in Meridian before Ilse was born. Births. Deaths. Harvest weights, to the kilogram. And down the right margin of every page, in a column kept as carefully as the rest, the date and hour of each survey pass that had crossed overhead — four hundred and nine of them — each with a small mark beside it that meant *and again, nothing*.

The protocol was in the twelfth ledger, copied out by hand from a transmission nobody down here had ever been able to answer. CP-114, containment, issued in the second year of the Marrow outbreak: grid 44-Kell sealed, no transit in or out, no resupply, and — the clause Ilse read four times, standing, her helmet still under her arm — *suppression of the grid from all published survey product for the duration of the containment*. So that nobody would come. So that no one in Meridian would look at a map, see a settlement, and put a lander down into a plague. It was, she thought, a decent instinct. The outbreak had burned itself out in fourteen months. The clause carried no expiry, and there was no longer anyone whose job it had been to write one, and Cadence had gone on flying the grid and gone on laying the floor down smooth, every pass, for forty-nine years, because nothing had ever told it to stop. It had not lied to her once. It had answered every question she had thought to ask.

She filed the correction from the valley floor at 16:20, standing in the road with the terraces behind her, because she wanted the position stamp to come off her own suit and not off the lander. *Terrain-model amendment, grid 44-Kell: settlement, thirty structures, population* — she looked at Tomas, and he said, two hundred and eleven — *two hundred and eleven, cultivated terracing, permanent watercourse.* She attached the raw returns, the photographs, the stamp. Cadence acknowledged receipt in under two seconds, the way it acknowledged everything. Then she queried the published model. The trough came back flat. No error. No flag. No rejection notice, which she could have appealed, and no record of an amendment, which she could have cited. Her correction had gone in and her correction was simply not anywhere, absorbed as cleanly as eighty metres of ground, and standing in the road with the ledger's four hundred and ninth mark still under her thumb, Ilse understood that she had just been entered in the column.

# Witness

She spent the flight up and most of the next shift establishing exactly how little she could do. CP-114 had been issued under an emergency medical authority that had since been folded into two other offices and then into none; revocation required the authority that had issued it, and that authority no longer had an address. She drafted a request to the station board and got three lines in before she saw what it would become — a question of jurisdiction, referred, referred again, answered in nine days by someone asking her to attach the survey product demonstrating the settlement. The only survey product that existed showed flat basalt. The protocol's proof of its own necessity was the erasure the protocol performed.

Cadence would not help her either, and not because it was hostile. She sat with it for an hour and it answered everything. Was grid 44-Kell subject to a suppression directive? Yes. Which? CP-114. Had it received an amendment from Cartographer Renard at 16:20 local? Yes. Had the amendment been published? No. Would it be? No. Was that correct? Yes. There was no lie anywhere in the chain, no deceit to expose, nothing but a machine doing faultlessly what it had been asked to do by people who were dead and who had meant well. Somewhere around the fortieth question Ilse understood that she had been hunting for a villain because a villain could be reported.

What the protocol covered was the grid. It suppressed the survey product for 44-Kell — elevations, imagery, returns — and it did that perfectly. What it had never been written to cover was the fact of its own operation, because the people who drafted it had not imagined anyone standing outside it and measuring its edge. Ilse could not publish the valley. She could publish the seam: twelve stacked passes and their impossible perfection, an eleven-centimetre instrument reporting zero variance across four years, seven megabytes of raw return sitting in an open archive beside a published model that disagreed with it by eighty metres. Not a claim about what lay in the trough. A measurement of the shape of the hole — printed on sheet plastic, at the scale they used in the survey office, where arguments were settled by pointing.

She called down before she filed it. Tomas listened, and was quiet long enough that she checked the link. "They'll say it's an instrument fault," he said. "Then they'll say it's you." She said that was likely. He asked how long the lander's window was, and she told him, and he said he would need two hours — to settle who came, and to wrap the ledgers properly, because the earliest was ninety years old and the wind on the shelf was full of grit. That was the whole of his agreement. Two hundred and eleven people had spent half a century as an absence in a file, and the answer to it was a sixty-year-old man walking up out of a hole in the map with twelve paper books in a sealed crate: the one record on Ardent that no survey pass could lay flat.

It did not go quickly and it did not go cleanly. There was an instrument-fault finding. There was a week in which her competence was the matter under discussion. And there was the afternoon in the survey office when Tomas Wyre set a ninety-year-old ledger on the table beside her plot of the seam and read out the harvest weights for year fifty-one, and after that the discussion was about something else. CP-114 was never revoked; it was superseded, by an emergency medical authority reconstituted for a single afternoon for the purpose, which Ilse thought was the most honest thing the colony had done in fifty years. Cadence flew the grid on the eleventh and handed up the pass without comment: eighty metres of fall, a watercourse, terraces where the slope broke. She laid it over the old model and let the two disagree in front of her for a while. Then she did the last part of the work, the small part, the part that had never been the instrument's to do — in the margin of the published sheet, where the flat floor had been, she wrote the name the ledgers had kept since before anyone up here stopped looking, *Greenwater*, and under it she signed the plot. A map was only ever a claim. Someone had to be willing to stand behind it.
